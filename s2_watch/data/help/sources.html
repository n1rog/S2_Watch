Sources

S2 Watch reads six radio sources and about twenty official and open feeds. Radio sources are set under Configuration → Sources…; the feeds are switched on and off under Configuration → Watch Forms.

APRSBulletins (BLN0–BLN9 and named groups) from APRS-IS, receive-only, from stations in your coverage Tick Read APRS bulletins and enter your call sign with an SSID, e.g. N0ABC-13. Servers refuse anonymous logins; nothing is sent to RF.

Review existing feeder data from is the watch period: how far back S2 Watch reads each application's history when it starts, and how far back the map, the list under it and a shift briefing reach. Choose 3, 6, 12 or 24 hours, 2 or 5 days, 1 or 2 weeks, or 1 month; it is also on the Map tab as Watch period. 3 hours keeps the picture to the current shift's latest activity. APRS-IS has no history: you see bulletins from the moment S2 Watch connects.

A date in the future is a mistake. When a source dates a message more than 15 minutes ahead of this computer's clock, S2 Watch dates it when it was read instead, so a typo can't keep a report at the top of the lists for weeks.

Example. In October the State Department feed dated a French Polynesia advisory 02 Dec 2026 (it meant 2024). It is shown as read on the day S2 Watch fetched it. Reports saved with a future date by earlier versions are repaired the same way.

APRS bulletins carry their station's position. A bulletin has no position of its own, so S2 Watch also listens for the position beacons of each station that sends one, and adds the station's latitude and longitude to the bulletin. A station's first bulletin waits up to 10 minutes for its next beacon, then comes through without a position (stations that send only Mic-E beacons, mostly mobiles, aren't read). On the map, a place the bulletin names comes first; the station's position is used only when it names none, since it shows where the sender is, not where the news is.

Example.

W5ABC-10: BLN1: Net tonight 1900 [W5ABC-10 position: lat 36.1540, lon -95.9928]     → map: Station W5ABC-10 (Tulsa)
W5ABC-10: BLN2: Flooding in Joplin, MO [W5ABC-10 position: lat 36.1540, lon -95.9928] → map: Joplin, MO

Official and open sources (OSINT)

NWS alerts, storm reports and river gauges; the National Hurricane Center; USGS earthquakes and volcanoes; NOAA SPC storm reports; NIFC wildfires; FEMA declarations and local public alerts (IPAWS, placed at the area the alert outlines and named with its county, e.g. Sedona 8-9-10, Coconino County, AZ); NOAA space weather; FAA security restrictions, airport closures and ground stops; State Department advisories; internet outages (IODA); news about unrest and violence, and about failing infrastructure, from two news indexes, GDELT and Google News (see below). Off by default: worldwide disasters (GDACS), newly exploited software flaws (CISA), closed border crossings (CBP) and satellite heat detections (NASA FIRMS, which needs a free map key entered under Sources). FIRMS reports clusters of 6 or more detections in the last 24 hours at low urgency, worded “a fire, a field burn or industrial heat”: in season most are crop or sugarcane burns, steel mills or volcanoes, not wildfires.

Each source has a switch, how often it is fetched, and its status: Configuration → Watch Forms (the submenu or the page). Turning one off stops it at once and takes its items off the map; reports already saved stay until they leave the watch period.

Internet outages count only when service itself drops: at most 80% of a state's (or country's) networks reachable, or at most 90% of them announced. IODA calls far smaller dips “critical”, and its darknet and Google traffic signals swing without any outage, so those alone are never reported.

Example. Internet outage: Haiti — reachable networks at 72% of normal is reported; District of Columbia — announced networks at 99% of normal and Washington — darknet traffic at 8% of normal aren't.

Infrastructure news: where matters more than how many

A second news search looks for power outages, people without power, water main breaks, gas leaks, manhole fires, sinkholes, bridge collapses and rolling blackouts, in both GDELT and Google News (every 30 minutes each; either can be switched off under Configuration → Watch Forms). Two indexes because one can fail for days: GDELT refused every request from late October into November. Google News covers local TV and newspapers within minutes, but its U.S. edition carries world news too: a story whose place S2 Watch can't find is kept only when it comes from a U.S. website and names no other country or nationality. Each story becomes an Infrastructure report whose urgency comes from where it is rather than from how many customers lost service:

UrgencyWhen
HighIt names a critical site: a hospital, airport, military base, the White House, Capitol or Pentagon, a federal building or embassy, police, fire, 911 or emergency operations, a water or power plant, a data center, or Metro, subway or other transit. Or it is anywhere in Washington, DC. Or it is very large: 100,000 customers or more, statewide, rolling blackouts, a grid emergency or a state of emergency.
MediumIt is in a state capital, or affects 10,000 customers or more, thousands of people, a whole city or county, or schools and universities.
LowThe rest: a neighbourhood losing power for a while.

A headline that names no place is placed by its story's web address, which often ends with it (…-h-street-northwest-dc).

Example. Power outages hit Foggy Bottom as crews investigate smoke from manholes (WJLA, about 1,200 Pepco customers) → Infrastructure, high, Washington, DC, because it is in the national capital. Power outage leaves 2,000 without power in Tulsa → low. Thousands without power in Columbus, Ohio → medium (a state capital).

Washington, DC is recognised however it is written: Washington, DC or D.C., “DC’s”, “in DC”, “Northwest DC”, “DC police”, the Washington Monument, Navy Yard and George Washington University, and neighbourhoods no other city has (Foggy Bottom, Dupont Circle, Adams Morgan, Anacostia, Logan Circle, Tenleytown, Congress Heights, the National Mall). “Washington” alone is still the state, and “12V DC” isn't a place.

Coverage: which parts of the world

Configuration → Coverage chooses the areas S2 Watch reports from: United States (the default), North America (Canada, Mexico, Greenland), Central America and the Caribbean, South America, Europe, Africa, Asia and the Middle East, Oceania and Antarctica. Tick as many as you like; one always stays ticked.

  • Worldwide sources follow it: USGS earthquakes (M4.5+ in covered areas; M6+ anywhere always), GDELT news (U.S. news alone while only the U.S. is covered; English news about the covered areas otherwise), GDACS disasters (switched on when you first add an area beyond the U.S.), hurricanes, and internet outages (by state in the U.S., by country elsewhere).
  • U.S. sources (NWS, SPC, NIFC, FEMA, FAA, IPAWS, river gauges, NASA FIRMS, CBP) pause while the U.S. isn't covered.
  • Radio traffic is never dropped for being about somewhere else: your network's reports are always kept. Messages naming a country or a major city abroad are placed there on the map.
  • What's on screen follows it too. Untick an area and the map markers, the rows under the map and the events located there hide at once, including ones received earlier; tick it again and they come back. Nothing is deleted, and a message that names no place always shows.
  • APRS follows it by station. APRS-IS carries the whole world, so a bulletin is kept only when its station is in a covered area: placed by its position beacon, or else a U.S. call sign counts as the U.S. A station abroad that sends no beacon can't be placed and is dropped unless every area is ticked. Bulletins already on screen hide the same way.
  • State Department travel advisories always show: they are U.S. guidance about other countries.

Example. Tick Europe and Asia and the Middle East. Within a few minutes you get USGS: M4.9 earthquake — 53 km WNW of Leni, Italy, news such as Drone attack on Kyiv overnight placed at Kyiv, Ukraine, and Internet outage: North Korea. A message from K9DGY, QST YEMEN … SAUDI-LED COALITION AIRSTRIKES, is placed in Yemen. The list's heading shows the coverage, and briefings name it as their geographic focus.

Example. With United States and North America ticked, DO4DR-10: BLN1WXERZ: DWD WARNUNG vor FROST in ERZ isn't kept: its station's beacon puts it in Germany. DB0RVB: BLN1WXGOE: DWD WARNUNG vor FROST sends no beacon and its call sign isn't a U.S. one, so it isn't kept either. VE3XYZ: BLN1: Ice storm in Ottawa, beaconing from Canada, is.

Places abroad are found by country name or by city (about 1,900 cities of 200,000 people or more, and every capital). U.S. places always come first: “Paris, TX” stays in Texas, “Lebanon, PA” in Pennsylvania, and a name such as Chad, Jordan or Turkey counts as a country only after “in”, “from”, “near” or similar. A city whose name is also an everyday word (Hail, Nice, Male, Van, Split, Reading, Tours) counts only with its country named: “HAIL REPORTED” and “MALE SUSPECT” stay where they are, “Nice, France” is Nice.

  • A headline's outlet isn't a place. The newspaper's name after a “|” (or after a dash, when it matches the sender's site) counts only when the headline names no place itself: “French protests kick off | The Peninsula Qatar” is not about Qatar, while “Oak Brook police use plate readers – NBC Chicago” stays near Chicago.
  • Nationality words place national news when the headline names no place: “French protests”, “Iranian military”, “German government” go to that country. They count only before such a word, so “French Quarter” or “Chinese restaurant” don't.
  • A headline that names only a country puts the report at the middle of the country. If you know the city (the article says Paris), use Fix location in the event's details.

Example.

Police fire tear gas as new French protests kick off | The Peninsula Qatar   → France
Israel orders lockdown of West Bank – NBC Los Angeles                       → Israel
Storm floods French Quarter in New Orleans | WWL                            → New Orleans, LA

Status lights

Configuration → Sources… shows one light per source: green connected, red not connected, grey not monitored (no watch form uses it) or switched off. The status bar at the bottom says the same in a line.

Example. The status bar reads OSINT: Connected (faa, state, …, volcanoes); failing: gdelt.

Every OSINT source works except GDELT news, which refused the request (often “429 Too Many Requests”). S2 Watch waits 30 minutes before asking again; nothing needs doing.

Example. FEMA's public alerts (IPAWS) never appear.

FEMA's archive runs about a day behind, so with a 24-hour watch period every alert arrives already too old. Set the watch period to 2 days or more to see them.
